This study introduces a fungal aromatic prenyltransferase AscC into the engineered <italic toggle="yes">Escherichia coli</italic> to catalyze the transfer of C<sub>5</sub>-C<sub>15</sub> terpenoid linear precursors to olivetolic acid. Four CBGA derivatives (compounds <bold>1–4</bold>) with diverse C<sub>5</sub>, C<sub>10</sub>, or C<sub>15</sub> prenyl chains are isolated and identified, with compound <bold>4</bold> being an undescribed product featuring a C<sub>15</sub> prenyl chain at the C-5 position. Compound <bold>4</bold> demonstrates the highest anti-neuroinflammatory and antibacterial activities, with IC<sub>50</sub> values of 3.06 µM for TNF-α and 4.31 µM for IL-6, alongside EC<sub>50</sub> values ranging from 0.87 to 3.16 µM against three Gram-positive bacteria. An efficient construct is established by incorporating an additional copy of AscC, resulting in a yield of 14.85 ± 0.91 mg/L of compound <bold>4</bold>. Two mutants, L180Y and L180F, are engineered to selectively produce compound <bold>4</bold>. 2025</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec id="Sec1" sec-type="introduction"><title>Introduction</title><p id="Par4">Natural products play pivotal roles in chemical biology and drug discovery due to their unique chemical structures and specific biological targets<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref></sup>. The construction of natural product analogs is important for exploring structure-activity relationships, developing drugs with enhanced bioactivity and bioavailability, and uncovering disease mechanisms as chemical biology tools<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref></sup>. With the advances in synthetic biology, combinatorial biosynthesis exploiting enzymatic and pathway promiscuity provides an alternative approach to generate novel natural product analogs<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref></sup>.</p><p id="Par5">Meroterpenoids represent a class of natural products with conspicuous biological activities derived from the terpenoid biosynthetic pathway coupled with other biogenic pathways. Among these, cannabinoids stand out as unique meroterpenoids initially isolated from the plant <italic toggle="yes">Cannabis sativa</italic>, a herbal medicine with a history spanning over 3500 years<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref></sup>. More than 120 phytocannabinoids have been isolated from <italic toggle="yes">Cannabis</italic>, most of which are alkyl resorcinol derivatives with a monoterpenoid unit<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref></sup>. Notably, cannabinoids, such as Δ<sup>9</sup>-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD), exhibit potent pharmacological effects, including analgesic, antiemetic, anti-spasmodic, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activities<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref></sup>, which renders them of great medicinal importance<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref></sup>. The pharmacological activities of cannabinoids principally occur through interacting with cannabinoid receptors CB1 and CB2, belonging to the superfamily of G-protein coupled receptors which mediate most of physiological responses and so have great potential as therapeutic targets for a broad spectrum of diseases<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR13">13</xref></sup>. The important influence of the C-3 side chain of cannabinoids on the pharmacological activities has been identified through chemical synthesis of a series of C-3 alkyl analogs<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref></sup>, however, the effect of the prenyl chain on pharmaceutical properties remains unexplored.</p><p id="Par6">The biosynthesis of cannabinoids is derived from a monoterpenoid unit produced by 2-<italic toggle="yes">C</italic>-methyl-D-erythritol 4-phosphate (MEP) pathway and an aromatic moiety olivetolic acid (OA) catalyzed by an olivetol synthase and an OA cyclase using malonyl-CoA and hexanoyl-CoA as substrates<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref></sup>. Cannabigerolic acid (CBGA) is the committed biosynthetic precursor of cannabinoids, formed by a C-C Friedel-Craft alkylation of OA with geranyl diphosphate (C<sub>10</sub>, GDP)<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR18">18</xref></sup>. Subsequently, the geranyl moiety of CBGA undergoes cyclization reaction to form cannabinoids such as Δ<sup>9</sup>-tetrahydrocannabinolic acid (THCA), cannabidiolic acid (CBDA), or cannabichromenic acid (CBCA), which can be decarboxylated to their neutral forms (Fig. <xref rid="Fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref>)<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref></sup>.<fig id="Fig1" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 1</label><caption><p>The biosynthetic pathway of cannabinoids.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="d33e348" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.jpg"><?image-name 42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.jpg?><?image-size 76817?><?image-md5 eebf2bdab2c685ba9ed413d6cb2d1e30?><?image-image-server-status LOAD_COMPLETED?><?image-original-height 1463?><?image-original-width 1749?><?image-scaled-height 585?><?image-scaled-width 699?><?image-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/0f1f/11794439/eebf2bdab2c6/42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.jpg?><?thumb-name 42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.gif?><?thumb-size 2956?><?thumb-md5 6f7d19eca7bba26f14b9d37584c1bafd?><?thumb-image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?><?thumb-scaled-height 84?><?thumb-scaled-width 100?><?thumb-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/0f1f/11794439/6f7d19eca7bb/42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.gif?></graphic></fig></p><p id="Par7">Heterologous biosynthesis of cannabinoids has been achieved in engineered yeasts, paving an alternative way for access to cannabinoids<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref></sup>. Based on enzyme promiscuity and engineered biosynthetic pathways, a series of unnatural cannabinoid analogs with distinct alkyl moieties at the C-3 position were obtained by combinatorial biosynthesis through feeding different fatty acids<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref></sup>. Given the diversity of terpenoids which includes hemiterpenoids, monoterpenoids, sesquiterpenoids, diterpenoids, and sesterterpenoids, we are particularly interested in the biosynthesis of CBGA analogs with distinct terpenoid chains. Several aromatic prenyltransferases have been reported, among which AscC, a UbiA-type prenyltransferase from <italic toggle="yes">Fusarium</italic> sp. NBRC 100844, could catalyze the conversion of 2-methyl-4,6-dihydroxy benzaldehyde into the corresponding farnesylated product<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref></sup>. In this work, we synthesized four CBGA analogs <bold>1–4</bold> with a C<sub>5</sub>, C<sub>10</sub>, or C<sub>15</sub> prenyl unit using the engineered <italic toggle="yes">Escherichia coli</italic> harboring the AscC gene. Molecular docking and site-directed mutagenesis resulted in two mutants selectively producing the undescribed compoud <bold>4</bold>. In addition, the anti-neuroinflammatory and antibacterial activities of these compounds were observed. </p></sec><sec id="Sec2" sec-type="results"><title>Results</title><sec id="Sec3"><title>Heterologous expression of promiscuous aromatic prenyltransferases in engineered <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> strains for cannabinoid analogs production</title><p id="Par8">To biosynthesize cannabinoid analogs featuring diverse prenyl side chains, the engineered <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> strains were recruited, which contain an exogenous MVA pathway along with a farnesyl diphosphate synthase (FDPS) or geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGDPS) or geranylfarnesyl diphosphate synthase (GFDPS) previously constructed for terpenoid biosynthesis<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref></sup>. The aromatic prenyltransferase (CsPT4) from <italic toggle="yes">C. sativa</italic> and four fungal promiscuous aromatic prenyltransferases (aPTases), including AscC from <italic toggle="yes">Fusarium</italic> sp., Trt2 and AtaPT from <italic toggle="yes">Aspergillus terreus</italic>, and Orf2 from <italic toggle="yes">Streptomyces</italic> sp.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref></sup>. were picked out to test their activity for transferring various prenyl groups to the aromatic ring of OA (Supplementary Fig. <xref rid="MOESM1" ref-type="media">1</xref>). The full-length cDNA of these aPTases was synthesized after codon optimization and then constructed into the corresponding expression vector.</p><p id="Par9">The four fungal aromatic prenyltransferases could express substantial amounts of recombinant proteins with expected molecular mass in the soluble fraction. These aPTases were subsequently transformed into the engineered <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> strains producing C<sub>5</sub>-C<sub>25</sub> prenyl diphosphates. By feeding with OA, four specific peaks could be detected in the extract of engineered <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> containing AscC using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref>). The pseudo-molecular ion peaks ([M-H]<sup>–</sup>) of specific products were 291.1601, 359.2226, 427.2853, and 427.2851, indicating that AscC could catalyze the introduction of C<sub>5</sub>, C<sub>10</sub>, and C<sub>15</sub> prenyl chains into OA (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref>). The conversion rate of OA was 70.48% by AscC. By contrast, no obvious product was observed in the engineered bacteria harboring Trt2, AtaPT, and Orf2. In addition, we tested the catalytic activity of AscC towards a series of OA analogs (<bold>S1</bold>-<bold>S7</bold>), but no products were observed (Supplementary Fig. <xref rid="MOESM1" ref-type="media">2</xref>). Compared to OA, these analogs lack a hydroxyl group at the C-4 position, except <bold>S4</bold> that has an ester group at the C-1 position instead of a carboxyl group. To elucidate the structure of cannabinoid analogs, a 10 L scale fermentation of the engineered BPA1 strain was performed and extracted with ethyl acetate. The <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> extracts were further purified with a combination of repeated silica-gel and Sephadex LH-20 column chromatography. Cannabinoids represent a unique class of natural products with substantial pharmaceutical values. Notable efforts have focused on the generation of cannabinoid analogs with distinct alkyl moieties, such as combinatorial biosynthesis of unnatural cannabinoid analogs through feeding different fatty acids<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref></sup>. In this study, cannabinoid analogs with varied terpenoid chains were created by introducing a promiscuous fungal aromatic prenyltransferase AscC into engineered bacteria strains capable of terpenoid production. The most abundant product, compound <bold>4</bold>, is an undescribed compound featuring a C<sub>15</sub> prenyl chain at the C-5 position of olivetolic acid.</p><p id="Par19">Considering the steric hindrance of the <italic toggle="yes">n</italic>-pentyl group and the electron-donating effect of the hydroxyl groups in the aromatic ring, prenylation of OA would theoretically favor the C-3 position over the C-5 position. However, when using farnesyl diphosphate (FDP) as the prenyl donor, the amount of compound <bold>4</bold> prenylated at the C-5 position was significantly higher than that of compound <bold>3</bold> at the C-3 position. Notably, C-5 substituted cannabinoids are rare, with the exception of 5-geranyl olivetolate isolated from <italic toggle="yes">Helichrysum umbraculigerum</italic><sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref></sup>. A number of aPTases responsible for catalyzing the attachment of prenyl groups to aromatic substrates have been reported. For instance, UbiA-type PTases CsPT1 and CsPT4 reported from <italic toggle="yes">Cannabis</italic> catalyze the attachment of a geranyl group to OA<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref></sup>, and NphB (formerly Orf2) identified from <italic toggle="yes">Streptomyces</italic> sp. strain CL190 could catalyze the attachment of a geranyl group to aromatic substrates<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR28">28</xref></sup>. Recently, the core cannabinoid biosynthetic enzymes have also been identified from <italic toggle="yes">H. umbraculigerum</italic>, including four aPTases HuPT1-4 among that HuPT4 (HuCBGAS4) geranylated the aromatic dihydrostilbenic acid<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR38">38</xref></sup>. Notably, these identified aPTases predominantly catalyze prenylation at the C-3 position of aromatic substrates<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR31">31</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref></sup>. By contrast, AscC can catalyze OA to produce farnesylated products at both the C-3 and C-5 positions, suggesting a distinct catalytic mechanism. The unusual regioselectivity of AscC warrants further investigation, and the rise of artificial intelligence and language modes will help enzyme engineering in future<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR40">40</xref></sup>. The unique structure of compound <bold>4</bold> presents significant challenges for regioselective chemical synthesis. Guided by structure-based enzyme engineering, we successfully improved the selectivity of AscC, and developed two variants, L180Y and L180F, which exclusively produce compound <bold>4</bold>. These findings provide promising catalysts for manipulating the production of compound <bold>4</bold> using synthetic biology.</p><p id="Par20">We found that compounds <bold>1–4</bold> exerted significant anti-neuroinflammatory and antimicrobial properties, with compound <bold>4</bold> being the most effective. This indicates that the prenyl side chains at the C-5 position of OA play crucial roles in these activities. Additionally, novel cannabinoids with remarkable bioactivities could potentially be generated via prenyl cyclization. For instance, CBD demonstrates enhanced anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activity compared to CBGA<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR42">42</xref></sup>. Further efforts should focus on exploring more cyclases and expression systems to expand the chemical space of cannabinoids.</p></sec><sec id="Sec9"><title>Methods</title><sec id="Sec10"><title>General experimental procedures</title><p id="Par21">Column chromatography was performed on silica gel (200 − 300 mesh, Qingdao Dingkang Silicone Co., Ltd., P. Compound <bold>4</bold> demonstrates the highest anti-neuroinflammatory and antibacterial activities, with IC<sub>50</sub> values of 3.06 µM for TNF-α and 4.31 µM for IL-6, alongside EC<sub>50</sub> values ranging from 0.87 to 3.16 µM against three Gram-positive bacteria. An efficient construct is established by incorporating an additional copy of AscC, resulting in a yield of 14.85 ± 0.91 mg/L of compound <bold>4</bold>. Two mutants, L180Y and L180F, are engineered to selectively produce compound <bold>4</bold>. These findings provide a foundation for enriching the chemical diversity of bioactive cannabinoid analogs with various prenyl moieties through combinatorial biosynthesis.</p><p id="Par2"> <graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="anchor" id="d33e246" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="42003_2025_7509_Figa_HTML.jpg"><?image-name 42003_2025_7509_Figa_HTML.jpg?><?image-size 46447?><?image-md5 87c6217a421b7c4bb939bc9c03e01fb2?><?image-image-server-status LOAD_COMPLETED?><?image-original-height 467?><?image-original-width 950?><?image-scaled-height 311?><?image-scaled-width 633?><?image-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/0f1f/11794439/87c6217a421b/42003_2025_7509_Figa_HTML.jpg?><?thumb-name 42003_2025_7509_Figa_HTML.gif?><?thumb-size 5423?><?thumb-md5 8294375f765c9425c4f449aa4790a9fd?><?thumb-image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?><?thumb-scaled-height 80?><?thumb-scaled-width 162?><?thumb-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/0f1f/11794439/8294375f765c/42003_2025_7509_Figa_HTML.gif?></graphic> </p></abstract><abstract id="Abs2" abstract-type="web-summary"><p id="Par3">Four cannabinoid derivatives are biosynthesized in engineered bacteria. 2025</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec id="Sec1" sec-type="introduction"><title>Introduction</title><p id="Par4">Natural products play pivotal roles in chemical biology and drug discovery due to their unique chemical structures and specific biological targets<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref></sup>. The construction of natural product analogs is important for exploring structure-activity relationships, developing drugs with enhanced bioactivity and bioavailability, and uncovering disease mechanisms as chemical biology tools<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref></sup>. With the advances in synthetic biology, combinatorial biosynthesis exploiting enzymatic and pathway promiscuity provides an alternative approach to generate novel natural product analogs<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref></sup>.</p><p id="Par5">Meroterpenoids represent a class of natural products with conspicuous biological activities derived from the terpenoid biosynthetic pathway coupled with other biogenic pathways. Among these, cannabinoids stand out as unique meroterpenoids initially isolated from the plant <italic toggle="yes">Cannabis sativa</italic>, a herbal medicine with a history spanning over 3500 years<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref></sup>. More than 120 phytocannabinoids have been isolated from <italic toggle="yes">Cannabis</italic>, most of which are alkyl resorcinol derivatives with a monoterpenoid unit<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref></sup>. Notably, cannabinoids, such as Δ<sup>9</sup>-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD), exhibit potent pharmacological effects, including analgesic, antiemetic, anti-spasmodic, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activities<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref></sup>, which renders them of great medicinal importance<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref></sup>. The pharmacological activities of cannabinoids principally occur through interacting with cannabinoid receptors CB1 and CB2, belonging to the superfamily of G-protein coupled receptors which mediate most of physiological responses and so have great potential as therapeutic targets for a broad spectrum of diseases<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR13">13</xref></sup>. The important influence of the C-3 side chain of cannabinoids on the pharmacological activities has been identified through chemical synthesis of a series of C-3 alkyl analogs<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref></sup>, however, the effect of the prenyl chain on pharmaceutical properties remains unexplored.</p><p id="Par6">The biosynthesis of cannabinoids is derived from a monoterpenoid unit produced by 2-<italic toggle="yes">C</italic>-methyl-D-erythritol 4-phosphate (MEP) pathway and an aromatic moiety olivetolic acid (OA) catalyzed by an olivetol synthase and an OA cyclase using malonyl-CoA and hexanoyl-CoA as substrates<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref></sup>. Cannabigerolic acid (CBGA) is the committed biosynthetic precursor of cannabinoids, formed by a C-C Friedel-Craft alkylation of OA with geranyl diphosphate (C<sub>10</sub>, GDP)<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR18">18</xref></sup>. Subsequently, the geranyl moiety of CBGA undergoes cyclization reaction to form cannabinoids such as Δ<sup>9</sup>-tetrahydrocannabinolic acid (THCA), cannabidiolic acid (CBDA), or cannabichromenic acid (CBCA), which can be decarboxylated to their neutral forms (Fig. <xref rid="Fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref>)<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref></sup>.<fig id="Fig1" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 1</label><caption><p>The biosynthetic pathway of cannabinoids.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="d33e348" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.jpg"><?image-name 42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.jpg?><?image-size 76817?><?image-md5 eebf2bdab2c685ba9ed413d6cb2d1e30?><?image-image-server-status LOAD_COMPLETED?><?image-original-height 1463?><?image-original-width 1749?><?image-scaled-height 585?><?image-scaled-width 699?><?image-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/0f1f/11794439/eebf2bdab2c6/42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.jpg?><?thumb-name 42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.gif?><?thumb-size 2956?><?thumb-md5 6f7d19eca7bba26f14b9d37584c1bafd?><?thumb-image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?><?thumb-scaled-height 84?><?thumb-scaled-width 100?><?thumb-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/0f1f/11794439/6f7d19eca7bb/42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.gif?></graphic></fig></p><p id="Par7">Heterologous biosynthesis of cannabinoids has been achieved in engineered yeasts, paving an alternative way for access to cannabinoids<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref></sup>. Based on enzyme promiscuity and engineered biosynthetic pathways, a series of unnatural cannabinoid analogs with distinct alkyl moieties at the C-3 position were obtained by combinatorial biosynthesis through feeding different fatty acids<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref></sup>. Given the diversity of terpenoids which includes hemiterpenoids, monoterpenoids, sesquiterpenoids, diterpenoids, and sesterterpenoids, we are particularly interested in the biosynthesis of CBGA analogs with distinct terpenoid chains. Several aromatic prenyltransferases have been reported, among which AscC, a UbiA-type prenyltransferase from <italic toggle="yes">Fusarium</italic> sp. NBRC 100844, could catalyze the conversion of 2-methyl-4,6-dihydroxy benzaldehyde into the corresponding farnesylated product<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref></sup>. In this work, we synthesized four CBGA analogs <bold>1–4</bold> with a C<sub>5</sub>, C<sub>10</sub>, or C<sub>15</sub> prenyl unit using the engineered <italic toggle="yes">Escherichia coli</italic> harboring the AscC gene. Molecular docking and site-directed mutagenesis resulted in two mutants selectively producing the undescribed compoud <bold>4</bold>. In addition, the anti-neuroinflammatory and antibacterial activities of these compounds were observed. </p></sec><sec id="Sec2" sec-type="results"><title>Results</title><sec id="Sec3"><title>Heterologous expression of promiscuous aromatic prenyltransferases in engineered <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> strains for cannabinoid analogs production</title><p id="Par8">To biosynthesize cannabinoid analogs featuring diverse prenyl side chains, the engineered <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> strains were recruited, which contain an exogenous MVA pathway along with a farnesyl diphosphate synthase (FDPS) or geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGDPS) or geranylfarnesyl diphosphate synthase (GFDPS) previously constructed for terpenoid biosynthesis<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref></sup>. The aromatic prenyltransferase (CsPT4) from <italic toggle="yes">C. sativa</italic> and four fungal promiscuous aromatic prenyltransferases (aPTases), including AscC from <italic toggle="yes">Fusarium</italic> sp., Trt2 and AtaPT from <italic toggle="yes">Aspergillus terreus</italic>, and Orf2 from <italic toggle="yes">Streptomyces</italic> sp.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref></sup>. were picked out to test their activity for transferring various prenyl groups to the aromatic ring of OA (Supplementary Fig. <xref rid="MOESM1" ref-type="media">1</xref>). The full-length cDNA of these aPTases was synthesized after codon optimization and then constructed into the corresponding expression vector.</p><p id="Par9">The four fungal aromatic prenyltransferases could express substantial amounts of recombinant proteins with expected molecular mass in the soluble fraction. These aPTases were subsequently transformed into the engineered <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> strains producing C<sub>5</sub>-C<sub>25</sub> prenyl diphosphates. Compared to OA, these analogs lack a hydroxyl group at the C-4 position, except <bold>S4</bold> that has an ester group at the C-1 position instead of a carboxyl group. Cannabinoids represent a unique class of natural products with substantial pharmaceutical values. Notable efforts have focused on the generation of cannabinoid analogs with distinct alkyl moieties, such as combinatorial biosynthesis of unnatural cannabinoid analogs through feeding different fatty acids<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref></sup>. In this study, cannabinoid analogs with varied terpenoid chains were created by introducing a promiscuous fungal aromatic prenyltransferase AscC into engineered bacteria strains capable of terpenoid production. The most abundant product, compound <bold>4</bold>, is an undescribed compound featuring a C<sub>15</sub> prenyl chain at the C-5 position of olivetolic acid.</p><p id="Par19">Considering the steric hindrance of the <italic toggle="yes">n</italic>-pentyl group and the electron-donating effect of the hydroxyl groups in the aromatic ring, prenylation of OA would theoretically favor the C-3 position over the C-5 position. However, when using farnesyl diphosphate (FDP) as the prenyl donor, the amount of compound <bold>4</bold> prenylated at the C-5 position was significantly higher than that of compound <bold>3</bold> at the C-3 position. Notably, C-5 substituted cannabinoids are rare, with the exception of 5-geranyl olivetolate isolated from <italic toggle="yes">Helichrysum umbraculigerum</italic><sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref></sup>. A number of aPTases responsible for catalyzing the attachment of prenyl groups to aromatic substrates have been reported. For instance, UbiA-type PTases CsPT1 and CsPT4 reported from <italic toggle="yes">Cannabis</italic> catalyze the attachment of a geranyl group to OA<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref></sup>, and NphB (formerly Orf2) identified from <italic toggle="yes">Streptomyces</italic> sp. strain CL190 could catalyze the attachment of a geranyl group to aromatic substrates<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR28">28</xref></sup>. Recently, the core cannabinoid biosynthetic enzymes have also been identified from <italic toggle="yes">H. umbraculigerum</italic>, including four aPTases HuPT1-4 among that HuPT4 (HuCBGAS4) geranylated the aromatic dihydrostilbenic acid<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR38">38</xref></sup>. Notably, these identified aPTases predominantly catalyze prenylation at the C-3 position of aromatic substrates<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR31">31</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref></sup>. By contrast, AscC can catalyze OA to produce farnesylated products at both the C-3 and C-5 positions, suggesting a distinct catalytic mechanism. The unusual regioselectivity of AscC warrants further investigation, and the rise of artificial intelligence and language modes will help enzyme engineering in future<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR40">40</xref></sup>. The unique structure of compound <bold>4</bold> presents significant challenges for regioselective chemical synthesis. Guided by structure-based enzyme engineering, we successfully improved the selectivity of AscC, and developed two variants, L180Y and L180F, which exclusively produce compound <bold>4</bold>. These findings provide promising catalysts for manipulating the production of compound <bold>4</bold> using synthetic biology.</p><p id="Par20">We found that compounds <bold>1–4</bold> exerted significant anti-neuroinflammatory and antimicrobial properties, with compound <bold>4</bold> being the most effective. This indicates that the prenyl side chains at the C-5 position of OA play crucial roles in these activities. Additionally, novel cannabinoids with remarkable bioactivities could potentially be generated via prenyl cyclization. For instance, CBD demonstrates enhanced anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activity compared to CBGA<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR42">42</xref></sup>. Further efforts should focus on exploring more cyclases and expression systems to expand the chemical space of cannabinoids.</p></sec><sec id="Sec9"><title>Methods</title><sec id="Sec10"><title>General experimental procedures</title><p id="Par21">Column chromatography was performed on silica gel (200 − 300 mesh, Qingdao Dingkang Silicone Co., Ltd., P. 2025</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec id="Sec1" sec-type="introduction"><title>Introduction</title><p id="Par4">Natural products play pivotal roles in chemical biology and drug discovery due to their unique chemical structures and specific biological targets<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref></sup>. The construction of natural product analogs is important for exploring structure-activity relationships, developing drugs with enhanced bioactivity and bioavailability, and uncovering disease mechanisms as chemical biology tools<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref></sup>. With the advances in synthetic biology, combinatorial biosynthesis exploiting enzymatic and pathway promiscuity provides an alternative approach to generate novel natural product analogs<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref></sup>.</p><p id="Par5">Meroterpenoids represent a class of natural products with conspicuous biological activities derived from the terpenoid biosynthetic pathway coupled with other biogenic pathways. Among these, cannabinoids stand out as unique meroterpenoids initially isolated from the plant <italic toggle="yes">Cannabis sativa</italic>, a herbal medicine with a history spanning over 3500 years<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref></sup>. More than 120 phytocannabinoids have been isolated from <italic toggle="yes">Cannabis</italic>, most of which are alkyl resorcinol derivatives with a monoterpenoid unit<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref></sup>. Notably, cannabinoids, such as Δ<sup>9</sup>-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD), exhibit potent pharmacological effects, including analgesic, antiemetic, anti-spasmodic, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activities<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR10">10</xref></sup>, which renders them of great medicinal importance<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref></sup>. The pharmacological activities of cannabinoids principally occur through interacting with cannabinoid receptors CB1 and CB2, belonging to the superfamily of G-protein coupled receptors which mediate most of physiological responses and so have great potential as therapeutic targets for a broad spectrum of diseases<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR13">13</xref></sup>. The important influence of the C-3 side chain of cannabinoids on the pharmacological activities has been identified through chemical synthesis of a series of C-3 alkyl analogs<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref></sup>, however, the effect of the prenyl chain on pharmaceutical properties remains unexplored.</p><p id="Par6">The biosynthesis of cannabinoids is derived from a monoterpenoid unit produced by 2-<italic toggle="yes">C</italic>-methyl-D-erythritol 4-phosphate (MEP) pathway and an aromatic moiety olivetolic acid (OA) catalyzed by an olivetol synthase and an OA cyclase using malonyl-CoA and hexanoyl-CoA as substrates<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref></sup>. Cannabigerolic acid (CBGA) is the committed biosynthetic precursor of cannabinoids, formed by a C-C Friedel-Craft alkylation of OA with geranyl diphosphate (C<sub>10</sub>, GDP)<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR18">18</xref></sup>. Subsequently, the geranyl moiety of CBGA undergoes cyclization reaction to form cannabinoids such as Δ<sup>9</sup>-tetrahydrocannabinolic acid (THCA), cannabidiolic acid (CBDA), or cannabichromenic acid (CBCA), which can be decarboxylated to their neutral forms (Fig. <xref rid="Fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref>)<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref></sup>.<fig id="Fig1" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 1</label><caption><p>The biosynthetic pathway of cannabinoids.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="d33e348" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.jpg"><?image-name 42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.jpg?><?image-size 76817?><?image-md5 eebf2bdab2c685ba9ed413d6cb2d1e30?><?image-image-server-status LOAD_COMPLETED?><?image-original-height 1463?><?image-original-width 1749?><?image-scaled-height 585?><?image-scaled-width 699?><?image-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/0f1f/11794439/eebf2bdab2c6/42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.jpg?><?thumb-name 42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.gif?><?thumb-size 2956?><?thumb-md5 6f7d19eca7bba26f14b9d37584c1bafd?><?thumb-image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?><?thumb-scaled-height 84?><?thumb-scaled-width 100?><?thumb-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/0f1f/11794439/6f7d19eca7bb/42003_2025_7509_Fig1_HTML.gif?></graphic></fig></p><p id="Par7">Heterologous biosynthesis of cannabinoids has been achieved in engineered yeasts, paving an alternative way for access to cannabinoids<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref></sup>. Based on enzyme promiscuity and engineered biosynthetic pathways, a series of unnatural cannabinoid analogs with distinct alkyl moieties at the C-3 position were obtained by combinatorial biosynthesis through feeding different fatty acids<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref></sup>. Given the diversity of terpenoids which includes hemiterpenoids, monoterpenoids, sesquiterpenoids, diterpenoids, and sesterterpenoids, we are particularly interested in the biosynthesis of CBGA analogs with distinct terpenoid chains. Several aromatic prenyltransferases have been reported, among which AscC, a UbiA-type prenyltransferase from <italic toggle="yes">Fusarium</italic> sp. NBRC 100844, could catalyze the conversion of 2-methyl-4,6-dihydroxy benzaldehyde into the corresponding farnesylated product<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref></sup>. In this work, we synthesized four CBGA analogs <bold>1–4</bold> with a C<sub>5</sub>, C<sub>10</sub>, or C<sub>15</sub> prenyl unit using the engineered <italic toggle="yes">Escherichia coli</italic> harboring the AscC gene. Molecular docking and site-directed mutagenesis resulted in two mutants selectively producing the undescribed compoud <bold>4</bold>. In addition, the anti-neuroinflammatory and antibacterial activities of these compounds were observed. </p></sec><sec id="Sec2" sec-type="results"><title>Results</title><sec id="Sec3"><title>Heterologous expression of promiscuous aromatic prenyltransferases in engineered <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> strains for cannabinoid analogs production</title><p id="Par8">To biosynthesize cannabinoid analogs featuring diverse prenyl side chains, the engineered <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> strains were recruited, which contain an exogenous MVA pathway along with a farnesyl diphosphate synthase (FDPS) or geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGDPS) or geranylfarnesyl diphosphate synthase (GFDPS) previously constructed for terpenoid biosynthesis<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref></sup>. The full-length cDNA of these aPTases was synthesized after codon optimization and then constructed into the corresponding expression vector.</p><p id="Par9">The four fungal aromatic prenyltransferases could express substantial amounts of recombinant proteins with expected molecular mass in the soluble fraction. In L180F, there were more interactions between the residues R41, G47, and R90 and the carboxyl or 2-hydroxyl group of OA (Fig. <xref rid="Fig5" ref-type="fig">5F, G</xref>), which likely reduced the binding flexibility of OA within the pocket. Moreover, a π-alkyl interaction between Y222 and the alkyl side chain of OA in L180F replaced the alkyl-alkyl interaction between M50 and the alkyl side chain of OA in AscC. Additional π-alkyl interactions between OA and FDP were also observed. Accordingly, the positioning of OA was likely stabilized within the enzyme pocket, facilitating regioselective Friedel-Craft alkylation at C-5 (Fig. <xref rid="Fig5" ref-type="fig">5D</xref>). In addition, the yields of compound <bold>4</bold> in variants L180Y and L180F reached up to 11.71 and 12.95 mg/L, respectively. The switch from an alkyl-alkyl to a π-alkyl interaction between the amino acid at position 180 and FDP, coupled with the additional π-alkyl interaction between Y218 and FDP, may provide enhanced cation stabilization. Besides, the reduced distance between C-5 and C-1 of FDP (from 6.7 to 6.2 Å) might also contribute to the improved conversion efficiency in variants L180Y and L180F. Furthermore, the natural substrates 2-methyl-4,6-dihydroxy benzaldehyde and FDP were also docked into the wild-type and mutant enzymes, respectively. The results suggested that L180F might still catalyze Friedel-Craft alkylation at the C-3 position, probably attributed to the methyl group at the C-1 position facilitating substrate flexibility within the enzyme pocket (Supplementary Fig. <xref rid="MOESM1" ref-type="media">17</xref>). However, the experiment was not conducted due to the unavailability of the substrate.</p><p id="Par17">The variants L176Y and L176F resulted in a substantial loss of enzymatic activity (Fig. <xref rid="Fig5" ref-type="fig">5C</xref>). Y222 interacted with FDP via π-alkyl interactions, and the mutants Y222L and Y222F exhibited a dramatic reduction in enzymatic activity, with 21% and 0.4% production of wild-type AscC, respectively. The residues R41, G47, and R90 were predicted to form hydrogen bonds or carbon interaction with OA, suggesting they play an important role in the binding and stabilizing of OA. This was confirmed by the substantial loss of enzymatic activity upon mutation R90E (Fig. <xref rid="Fig5" ref-type="fig">5C</xref>). The results highlighted the importance of these residues in enzyme catalysis.</p></sec></sec><sec id="Sec8" sec-type="discussion"><title>Discussion</title><p id="Par18">The development of natural product analogs holds significant promise in chemical biology and drug discovery. Cannabinoids represent a unique class of natural products with substantial pharmaceutical values. Notable efforts have focused on the generation of cannabinoid analogs with distinct alkyl moieties, such as combinatorial biosynthesis of unnatural cannabinoid analogs through feeding different fatty acids<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref></sup>. In this study, cannabinoid analogs with varied terpenoid chains were created by introducing a promiscuous fungal aromatic prenyltransferase AscC into engineered bacteria strains capable of terpenoid production. The most abundant product, compound <bold>4</bold>, is an undescribed compound featuring a C<sub>15</sub> prenyl chain at the C-5 position of olivetolic acid.</p><p id="Par19">Considering the steric hindrance of the <italic toggle="yes">n</italic>-pentyl group and the electron-donating effect of the hydroxyl groups in the aromatic ring, prenylation of OA would theoretically favor the C-3 position over the C-5 position. However, when using farnesyl diphosphate (FDP) as the prenyl donor, the amount of compound <bold>4</bold> prenylated at the C-5 position was significantly higher than that of compound <bold>3</bold> at the C-3 position. Notably, C-5 substituted cannabinoids are rare, with the exception of 5-geranyl olivetolate isolated from <italic toggle="yes">Helichrysum umbraculigerum</italic><sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref></sup>. A number of aPTases responsible for catalyzing the attachment of prenyl groups to aromatic substrates have been reported. For instance, UbiA-type PTases CsPT1 and CsPT4 reported from <italic toggle="yes">Cannabis</italic> catalyze the attachment of a geranyl group to OA<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref></sup>, and NphB (formerly Orf2) identified from <italic toggle="yes">Streptomyces</italic> sp. strain CL190 could catalyze the attachment of a geranyl group to aromatic substrates<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR28">28</xref></sup>. Recently, the core cannabinoid biosynthetic enzymes have also been identified from <italic toggle="yes">H. umbraculigerum</italic>, including four aPTases HuPT1-4 among that HuPT4 (HuCBGAS4) geranylated the aromatic dihydrostilbenic acid<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR38">38</xref></sup>. Notably, these identified aPTases predominantly catalyze prenylation at the C-3 position of aromatic substrates<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR31">31</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR39">39</xref></sup>. By contrast, AscC can catalyze OA to produce farnesylated products at both the C-3 and C-5 positions, suggesting a distinct catalytic mechanism. The unusual regioselectivity of AscC warrants further investigation, and the rise of artificial intelligence and language modes will help enzyme engineering in future<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR40">40</xref></sup>. The unique structure of compound <bold>4</bold> presents significant challenges for regioselective chemical synthesis. Guided by structure-based enzyme engineering, we successfully improved the selectivity of AscC, and developed two variants, L180Y and L180F, which exclusively produce compound <bold>4</bold>. These findings provide promising catalysts for manipulating the production of compound <bold>4</bold> using synthetic biology.</p><p id="Par20">We found that compounds <bold>1–4</bold> exerted significant anti-neuroinflammatory and antimicrobial properties, with compound <bold>4</bold> being the most effective. This indicates that the prenyl side chains at the C-5 position of OA play crucial roles in these activities. Additionally, novel cannabinoids with remarkable bioactivities could potentially be generated via prenyl cyclization. For instance, CBD demonstrates enhanced anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activity compared to CBGA<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR41">41</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR42">42</xref></sup>. Further efforts should focus on exploring more cyclases and expression systems to expand the chemical space of cannabinoids.</p></sec><sec id="Sec9"><title>Methods</title><sec id="Sec10"><title>General experimental procedures</title><p id="Par21">Column chromatography was performed on silica gel (200 − 300 mesh, Qingdao Dingkang Silicone Co., Ltd., P.
