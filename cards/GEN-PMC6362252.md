Because prenylated compounds often have a low natural abundance, alternative production methods are needed. Metabolic engineering enables natural product biosynthesis from inexpensive biomass, but is limited by the complexity of secondary metabolite pathways, intermediate and product toxicities, and substrate accessibility. Alternatively, enzyme catalyzed prenyl transfer provides excellent regio- and stereo-specificity, but requires expensive isoprenyl pyrophosphate substrates. Here we develop a flexible cell-free enzymatic prenylating system that generates isoprenyl pyrophosphate substrates from glucose to prenylate an array of natural products. The system provides an efficient route to cannabinoid precursors cannabigerolic acid (CBGA) and cannabigerovarinic acid (CBGVA) at &gt;1 g/L, and a single enzymatic step converts the precursors into cannabidiolic acid (CBDA) and cannabidivarinic acid (CBDVA). Cell-free methods may provide a powerful alternative to metabolic engineering for chemicals that are hard to produce in living organisms.</p></abstract><abstract id="Abs2" abstract-type="web-summary"><p id="Par2">Producing individual cannabinoids by metabolically engineered microbes has proven challenging. 2019</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec id="Sec1" sec-type="introduction"><title>Introduction</title><p id="Par3">Prenylated natural products are a large class of bioactive molecules with demonstrated medicinal properties<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref></sup>. Examples include prenyl-flavanoids, prenyl-stilbenoids, and cannabinoids (see Fig. <xref rid="Fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref>). Cannabinoids in particular show immense therapeutic potential with over 100 ongoing clinical trials as antiemetics, anticonvulsants, antidepressants, and analgesics<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR2">2</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref></sup>. Nevertheless, despite the therapeutic potential of prenyl-natural products, their study and use is limited by the lack of cost-effective production methods. Plant-derived prenyl-compounds are difficult to isolate due to the structural similarity of contaminating molecules, and the variable composition between crops<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref></sup>. These challenges are further exacerbated when attempting to isolate low abundance compounds. Many chemical syntheses have been developed to address the challenges associated with making prenylated natural products, but they are generally impractical for drug manufacturing due to the degree of complexity and low yields<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR11">11</xref></sup>.<fig id="Fig1" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 1</label><caption><p>The synthetic biochemistry platform for the production of prenyl-natural products. First, glucose is broken down into pyruvate through a glycolysis pathway modified to regulate NADPH levels (22) (12 enzymatic steps). Then, either PDH or the PDH bypass converts pyruvate into acetyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA is converted into GPP via the mevalonate pathway (eight enzymatic steps). By varying the aromatic prenyltransferase (aPT) and aromatic substrate we are able to produce various prenyl-flavonoids and prenyl-stilbenoids using the same central pathway. We developed variants of the prenyltransferase NphB (dNphB) to produce CBGA or CBGVA. CBGA is converted to cannabidiol and CBGVA is converted to cannabidivaric acid via cannabidiolic acid synthase (CBDAS). It is possible to produce other cannabinoids by using different cannabinoid synthases (THCAS and CBCAS)</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="d29e347" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="41467_2019_8448_Fig1_HTML.jpg"><?image-name 41467_2019_8448_Fig1_HTML.jpg?><?image-size 96704?><?image-md5 4575c47a8773f2ba3f717f0ecc79717a?><?image-image-server-status LOAD_COMPLETED?><?image-original-height 1726?><?image-original-width 978?><?image-scaled-height 1151?><?image-scaled-width 652?><?image-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/032d/6362252/4575c47a8773/41467_2019_8448_Fig1_HTML.jpg?><?thumb-name 41467_2019_8448_Fig1_HTML.gif?><?thumb-size 7889?><?thumb-md5 659bdd6f70e99e319e4289ad1192e194?><?thumb-image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?><?thumb-scaled-height 176?><?thumb-scaled-width 100?><?thumb-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/032d/6362252/659bdd6f70e9/41467_2019_8448_Fig1_HTML.gif?></graphic></fig></p><p id="Par4">Microbial production is a useful alternative to natural extraction for prenylated natural products, but comes with many challenges such as the need to divert carbon flux from central metabolism and product toxicity to name a few. For example, prenyl-natural products like prenyl-naringenin, prenyl-resveratrol, and cannabidiol (CBD) are derived from a combination of the metabolic pathways for fatty acid, isoprenoid, and polyketide biosynthesis. So, high-level production requires efficient re-routing of long, essential and highly regulated pathways. Despite the challenges, many groups have engineered microbes to produce unprenylated polyketides, like naringenin, resveratrol, and olivetolate, but at relatively low levels (110, 391, and 80 mg/L, respectively)<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR13">13</xref></sup>. Obtaining prenylated products is even more challenging because geranyl-pyrophosphate (GPP) is an essential metabolite that is toxic to cells at moderate concentrations, creating a significant barrier for high-level microbial production<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref></sup>. So, in spite of intense interest, to our knowledge there are no published reports of the complete biosynthesis of prenyl-flavonoids, prenyl-stilbenoids, or cannabinoids in recombinant microbes.</p><p id="Par5">Much recent effort has focused on alternative methods for cannabinoid production. Two groups have produced the polyketide cannabinoid intermediate, olivetolic acid (OA) at low levels in yeast (0.5 mg/L) or <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> (80 mg/L), but did not prenylate OA or produce a cannabinoid from the biosynthesized OA<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR13">13</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref></sup>. In other work, tetrahydrocannabinolic acid was produced in cell extracts from either exogenously added GPP and OA in a two enzyme pathway<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref></sup> or from cannabigerolic acid (CBGA) using a single enzyme<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref></sup>. However, it is unclear how GPP or CBGA could be obtained at sufficient levels for economical production due to the high cost of these molecules.</p><p id="Par6">Here, we propose an alternative biological approach to prenylated natural product biosynthesis using a cell-free enzymatic platform we call synthetic biochemistry, which has shown great promise for the production of bio-based molecules<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR18">18</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref></sup>. The synthetic biochemistry approach frees us from worrying about the toxicity of products and intermediates, affords rapid design-build-test cycles, precise control of all system components, and complete flexibility in pathway design. Nevertheless, building highly complex systems involving dozens of enzymes, associated cofactors and myriad metabolites on a large scale outside the context of the cell is an enormous challenge. One of the keys to making commercially viable cell-free systems is reducing enzyme costs by employing stable enzymes that can last for long periods of time. Recently, Zhang and co-workers converted maltodextrin into inositol at a 20,000 L scale in a five enzyme system using thermophilic enzymes purified by simple heating step<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref></sup>, demonstrating that at least simple cell-free systems can reach industrial scale. Another key requirement is designing systems that effectively generate and recycle high energy cofactors (ATP, NAD(P)H) so that they can be used many times. We have previously reported a flexible enzymatic purge valve and rheostat for regulating the supply of reducing equivalents and ATP<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR28">28</xref></sup>, allowing us to build systems that run for many days and produce high titers of isobutanol and terpenes. Here, we employ these concepts to develop cell-free production of a variety of prenylated compounds. We use glucose as a feedstock to produce GPP and optimize the system for the high-titer production of the cannabinoid compounds CBGA and cannabigerovarinic acid (CBGVA).</p></sec><sec id="Sec2" sec-type="results"><title>Results</title><sec id="Sec3"><title>Construction of the cell-free prenylation pathway</title><p id="Par7">Our synthetic biochemistry approach is outlined in Fig. <xref rid="Fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref> (detailed in Supplementary Figure <xref rid="MOESM1" ref-type="media">1</xref>) and expands on a system we developed previously for terpene production<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref></sup>. First, glucose is broken down via a modified glycolysis pathway to produce high-energy cofactors ATP and NADPH in addition to the carbon building block, acetyl-CoA using an alternative pyruvate oxidation pathway<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref></sup>. The acetyl-CoA is then assembled into the prenyl-donor compound, GPP, via the mevalonate pathway using the ATP and NADPH produced from glycolysis. Importantly, a purge valve<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref></sup> introduced into the glycolysis pathway balances NADPH production and consumption while maintaining carbon flux. The prenylation module then uses the GPP to prenylate exogenously added substrate to yield the desired prenylated product. To expand the capabilities of our synthetic biochemistry platform we developed a prenylating system that employs a nonspecific prenylating enzyme such as NphB, AtaPT, or NovQ to produce an array of prenyl-compounds derived from glucose<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR31">31</xref></sup>. We then further engineered NphB using Rosetta to specifically prenylate OA.</p><p id="Par8">As a first test of the system, we built the full cell-free system (23 enzymes) to generate GPP from glucose and employed wild-type NphB to prenylate its preferred substrate 1,6 dihydroxynapthalene (1,6 DHN; added exogenously). 1,6 DHN was added at the beginning of the reaction along with glucose. Up to ~400 mg/L (1.3 mM) of prenylated product was obtained from 2.5 mM 1,6 DHN. However, increasing the 1,6 DHN concentration from 2.5 to 5 mM, decreased final titers ~2-fold suggesting that 1,6 DHN inhibited one or more enzymes (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2a</xref>). Enzyme assays revealed that pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) was inhibited by 1,6 DHN, as well as olivetol, resveratrol, and olivetolate (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2b</xref>). Therefore, to engineer a general prenylation system, we sought to eliminate PDH.<fig id="Fig2" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 2</label><caption><p>Development of a PDH bypass for the prenylation of aromatic polyketides. <bold>a</bold> The comparison of the final titers achieved with the full pathway utilizing PDH (PDH system-gray trace) and the PDH bypass system (blue trace) at different concentrations of 1,6 DHN. (biological replicates, <italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 3). <bold>b</bold> The activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase (<italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> PDH) measured in the presence of various aromatic polyketides and 2% ethanol (vehicle) (biological replicates, <italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 3). <bold>c</bold> Various aromatic substrates were added to the pathway with either NphB, AtaPT, or NovQ prenyltransferase (biological replicates, <italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 3). In the PDH bypass, pyruvate is converted to acetyl-CoA using a pyruvate oxidase (PyOx) to produce acetyl-phosphate followed by the action of acetyl-phosphate transferase (PTA). The PDH bypass has two advantages. First, PDH is a large enzyme complex that is difficult to work with, so bypassing PDH streamlines enzyme production. More importantly, initial experiments revealed that the bypass is not subject to the inhibition seen at higher concentrations of 1,6 DHN. Once we confirmed the PDH bypass improved 1,6 DHN titers, we began to optimize the system as a general prenylation system. We varied co-factor concentrations, protein levels, and environmental conditions such as temperature and pH to identify the ideal set of conditions. Throughout this process we found that ATP, NADP<sup>+</sup>, phosphate, and NphB concentrations had the greatest impact on the final titer. As shown in Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2a</xref>, when we employed the PDH bypass, we found a 4-fold increase in titers of 5-prenyl-1,6 DHN when starting with 5 mM 1,6 DHN (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2a</xref>). When utilizing the PDH bypass system, approximately 50% of 1,6 DHN was converted in 24 h, reaching a final titer of 705 ± 12 mg/L (all error ranges in this work reflect the standard deviation) (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2b</xref>).</p><p id="Par10">We then tested the ability of the PDH bypass cell-free system to prenylate a variety of aromatic substrates (apigenin, diadzein, genestein, naringenin, olivetol, OA, and resveratrol). All the aromatic substrates were prenylated using one or more of the tested prenyl transferases (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2c</xref> and Supplementary Figures <xref rid="MOESM1" ref-type="media">5–10</xref>). Thus, it is possible to produce a variety of prenylated natural products using a cell-free enzymatic system to generate the expensive co-substrates GPP and DMAPP. Further, the ease with which an exogenous substrate can be added to a synthetic biochemistry system is a great advantage because it is often not possible to add co-substrates exogenously to microbes since they cannot enter the cell<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref></sup>.</p><p id="Par11">To test whether we could use synthetic biochemistry to produce high levels of therapeutically relevant prenylated products, we focused optimization efforts on cannabinoids due to the growing interest in new ways to make these medically important compounds. As shown in Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2d</xref> the initial system produced the cannabinoid precursor CBGA at a constant rate of 2.1 mg L<sup>−1</sup> h<sup>−1</sup> over 72 h and reached a final titer of only 132 mg/L (Fig. <xref rid="Fig2" ref-type="fig">2d</xref>).</p></sec><sec id="Sec4"><title>Redesign of NphB to improve CBGA synthesis</title><p id="Par12">Although the system produced CBGA, there were two problems. First, the turnover rate of the prenyltransferase NphB for CBGA production is extremely poor (<italic toggle="yes">k</italic><sub>cat</sub> = 0.0021 ± 0.00008 min<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup>, Supplementary Table <xref rid="MOESM1" ref-type="media">5</xref>). Second, prenylation of OA by NphB is highly nonspecific, generating a major side-product, 2-O-geranyl olivetolate<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR16">16</xref></sup>. We therefore, sought to improve CBGA production by enhancing the activity and specificity of NphB by design.</p><p id="Par13">Briefly, OA was docked into the active site of the NphB crystal structure (Fig. <xref rid="Fig3" ref-type="fig">3a</xref>)<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref></sup>, then Rosetta was used to predict mutations that would improve OA binding. Freedom from worries about cell viability allowed us to focus on pathway optimization rather than minimizing GPP toxicity, while the lack of a cell membrane barrier freed us to design a system with added aromatic molecules, which would not be possible in cells. Moreover, we could flexibly change the input from OA to DA to target rare cannabinoids without redesigning an entire pathway. Finally, it was straightforward to identify and focus our efforts on fixing the bottleneck steps. When we started this project we were only able to produce 9 mg/L of CBGA using the monoterpene pathway developed by Korman et al.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref></sup>. By introducing the PDH bypass and optimizing for cofactors, enzymes and environmental factors we were able to increase those titers to 132 mg/L. To improve titers further we engineered the NphB prenyltransferase, which further increased titers to 600 mg/L of CBGA. The final bottleneck was enzyme stability in the presence of CBGA, so by limiting the CBGA in the reaction vessel, we increased the titer to 1.25 g/L of CBGA, nearly a 140-fold improvement. Solutions were quickly implemented due to speedy design-build-test cycles, rapidly yielding results that far exceed published results using living cells. Like all new technology, the current system will need additional technical developments to become commercially viable, but our results suggest that synthetic biochemistry can become a realistic option for producing bio-based chemicals.</p></sec><sec id="Sec8"><title>Methods</title><sec id="Sec9"><title>Chemicals and reagents</title><p id="Par20">Yeast hexokinase and <italic toggle="yes">Corynebacterium glutamicum</italic> catalase were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. <italic toggle="yes">Aerococcus viridians</italic> pyruvate oxidase was purchased from A.G. scientific. The designed residues were in direct contact with the olivetolate and not in direct contact with GST. From the identical starting point each design was run five times using the -nstruct input.</p></sec><sec id="Sec19"><title>Initial NphB mutant library screening</title><p id="Par37">For screening of the initial library, we performed small scale expression and purifications. In total, 25 mL of LB media with 50 µg/mL of kanamycin was inoculated with 25 µL of a saturated culture of BL21 DE3 Gold harboring the NphB expression plasmid. The cultures were incubated at 37 °C until the OD<sub>600</sub> reached 0.4–0.6. The expression of the NphB constructs were induced with the addition of 1 mM IPTG, followed by incubation for 18 h at 18 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 2500 × <italic toggle="yes">g</italic>. The pellets were resuspended in 500 µL of lysis buffer: 50 mM [Tris pH 8.0], 150 mM NaCl, and 5 mM imidazole and lysed by sonication. The cell lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 20,000 × <italic toggle="yes">g</italic> for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was incubated at 4 °C with 50 µL of NiNTA resin. A 96-well spin column plate was used to purify the NphB constructs. The supernatant/resin was applied to the column and centrifuged for 2 min at 500 × <italic toggle="yes">g</italic>. A total of 500 µL of lysis buffer was then added, and the plate was centrifuged again for 1 min at 500 × <italic toggle="yes">g</italic>. The samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 16,060 × <italic toggle="yes">g</italic>, to remove precipitated proteins. The supernatant was analyzed using HPLC as described above.</p></sec><sec id="Sec21"><title>Enzyme kinetic parameters</title><p id="Par40">The reactions were set up under the following conditions: 50 mM Tris [pH 8.0], 2.5 mM GPP, 5 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, ~27 µM enzyme, and olivetolate or divarinic acid was varied from 0.1 to 6 mM in a final volume of 200 µL.
